\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Picking up an apple beside a post asks for three different decisions. The fingers must touch the apple, the wrist must avoid it, and the grasped apple must clear the post on its way to the basket. The same object therefore changes its role in collision checking as the task proceeds. We call the three decisions \emph{identity} (which object), \emph{extent} (which observed surface belongs to it), and \emph{permission} (which robot links may enter that surface, and for how long). Knowing the first does not settle the other two.

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies now produce such behaviors directly from pixels and language~\cite{brohan2023rt1,zitkovich2023rt2,kim2024openvla,black2024pi0,intelligence2025pi05}, but they compute no clearance, so geometric layers are placed behind them to keep their actions collision-free~\cite{wu2025momanipvla,hu2026vlsa,park2026knows}. Such layers inherit an assumption from motion planning~\cite{khatib1986potential,ratliff2009chomp,schulman2014trajopt}: every observed surface is something to avoid. Manipulation violates it by design, because the robot \emph{must} touch one surface, and which one changes with the sub-task. Placing a distance field behind a VLA is therefore not a matter of wiring. (i)~The field cannot tell the target from its neighbors. (ii)~The right treatment of the target depends on whether the robot is approaching, carrying, or releasing it. (iii)~Removing the target from the field hides it from every link, not only from the fingers. Current safety filters resolve (i) with an external open-vocabulary detector or segmenter~\cite{liu2024groundingdino,kirillov2023sam,wang2025yoloe} and approximate each object by an enclosing volume~\cite{hu2026vlsa,park2026knows}, while grounding-trained policies~\cite{huang2025roboground,vo2026obeyed} improve the policy's input but give the field nothing. An oversized volume frees the neighbor; an undersized one blocks the grasp. Neither error is repaired by a better detector or a stronger optimizer, because the field never received the right surface.

\textbf{Key idea.} The policy already answers the questions its safety layer is asking. Attention from the instruction's noun marks the object it is about to manipulate~\cite{kang2025fewheads,park2026knows}, and its hidden state tracks the stage of the task~\cite{bhardwaj2026progress}. What it lacks is geometry: a $16\times16$ attention patch has no metric boundary. Depth holds exactly the complementary knowledge; it shows where a surface is continuous and where it breaks, but not which surface the instruction means. We therefore keep the roles apart. Attention \emph{seeds}, depth continuity \emph{bounds}, centered internal features \emph{re-identify} the object across time, and physical evidence \emph{authorizes} attachment. Our development experiments support this division: once the geometry is right, fusing internal features into the surface boundary does not improve it, whereas comparing centered features across observations reliably tells whether a selected region is still the instructed object (Sec.~\ref{sec:featureresults}).

We present \sys, which turns this evidence into executable contact constraints for a frozen VLA (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). Attention-based Target Segmentation (ATS) grounds the target's identity and observed extent. Hidden-state Sub-task Recognition (HSR), together with gripper feedback and target--hand geometry, times the contact lifecycle. Link-specific Target Exemption (LTE) lets only designated contact links query a field from which the accepted target surface is removed, and turns a confirmed grasp into attached geometry. Constrained Chunk Refinement (CCR) tracks the policy's action chunk under these constraints and executes a verified prefix. In the example of Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}, the fingers may enter the apple's observed surface, the wrist may not, the post is never freed, and once HSR and the gripper agree that the apple is held, the apple joins the hand as geometry that the refiner keeps away from the post.

On 34 held-out episodes whose protocol and thresholds were frozen beforehand, the surface construction raises observed-surface IoU from \pend{0.773} to \pend{0.876} and improves \emph{every} episode; temporal reassociation lifts it to \pend{0.891} and frees \pend{92\%} less neighboring surface than radius growth, and the identity check flags \pend{85} of \pend{100} wrong selections. In closed loop, a frozen $\pi_{0.5}$ that is stopped by contact with a bar across the carry path in \pend{13} of \pend{14} hurdle episodes, completing none, completes \pend{7} with \sys\ and no bar contact; a pre-registered comparison on \tbd{40} unselected scenes, SafeLIBERO, and a real robot follow in Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}. Where the richer surface does not yet translate into higher success than our own coarser exemption, we say so and report what the diagnostics do and do not isolate.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Observed-surface grounding from a frozen VLA.} An attention-seeded, depth-continuous surface graph recovers the target's observed extent without an external segmenter, and a surface-matched robot mask keeps the observations that a volume mask discards.
\item \textbf{Evidence separation for identity, extent, and permission.} Geometry decides membership, centered internal features decide re-association and consistency, and physical evidence decides attachment; a controlled analysis shows where internal features help and where they do not.
\item \textbf{A task-conditioned contact representation.} Accepted target surfaces, interaction state, and designated contact links configure link-specific distance queries and attached geometry, connecting visual grounding to constrained action-chunk refinement, evaluated offline, in closed-loop simulation, \tbd{on SafeLIBERO, and on a real robot}.
\end{itemize}
